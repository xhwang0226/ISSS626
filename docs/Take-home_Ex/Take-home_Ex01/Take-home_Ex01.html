Take-home Exercise 1: Geospatial Analytics for Public Safety

Network-constrained and Spatio-temporal Point Patterns of Road Traffic Accidents in the Bangkok Metropolitan Region, 2022

Author

Wang Xiaohan

Published

September 23, 2026

Modified

September 27, 2026

View the Executive Summary (slides) →

1. Introduction

Road traffic crashes are a major public-safety problem in Thailand. WHO estimates about 18,218 road deaths in 2021, a rate of 25.4 per 100,000 population and one of the highest in Asia, with motorcyclists accounting for most deaths. The Bangkok Metropolitan Region (BMR), comprising Bangkok and five vicinity provinces, is the country’s largest urban area, where crash patterns are likely to vary strongly across space and time. However, a concentration of crashes does not necessarily mean higher risk, as it may also reflect traffic volume, road density or how crashes are recorded.

Road accidents can only happen on roads, so this study treats them as events on a road network. Following the Network Constrained Spatial Point Patterns Analysis (NetSPAA) approach, the main analysis uses network kernel density estimation (NKDE) for first-order properties and network K- and G-functions for second-order properties. Planar and spatio-temporal methods from earlier exercises are used to describe temporal variation and to compare planar and network results.

1.1 Research questions

  1. Where and when are road traffic accidents in BMR concentrated in 2022? (first-order)
  2. Are accidents clustered, regular or approximately random along the road network, compared with complete spatial randomness on the network? (second-order)
  3. Do conclusions change between planar and network-constrained views, and between bandwidths? (comparison and value-added)

Only roads intersecting BMR are read, because the full Thailand layer is over 600 MB; the spatial filter is converted to EPSG:4326 to match the OSM file. 4. Study Design

4.1 Point event definition

A point event is one recorded road traffic accident (one unique acc_code) that occurred between 1 January and 31 December 2022, located by its reported longitude and latitude, and falling inside BMR. For the network analysis, events must also lie within 100 m of a major road, and are snapped to the network by spNetwork. Events are treated as an unmarked point pattern: attributes such as fatalities or vehicle type are not used as weights.

4.3 Methodological decisions

Decision Choice Justification
CRS WGS 84 / UTM 47N (EPSG:32647) Metric units for bandwidth and distance; zone 47N covers Bangkok
Network OSM major roads (motorway to tertiary, with links) Matches the agencies that recorded the accidents
Lixels 500 m, minimum 250 m About half the NKDE bandwidth, giving detail without too many lixels
NKDE Quartic kernel, simple method, bandwidth 1 km (sensitivity: 0.5 and 2 km) Quartic and simple follow the NetSPAA workflow; 1 km covers junction-to-corridor scale
Network K/G-functions 0-5 km, step 100 m, width 200 m, 50 simulations, Bangkok only Range covers local to corridor scale; Bangkok only for computation time
Planar KDE Gaussian kernel; bw.diggle, bw.ppl, bw.scott, fixed 0.5-5 km, adaptive compared Used for comparison with the network view
Planar edge correction Border (default for large patterns) Fast for thousands of points
Planar simulations 99 Global envelopes with rank 1 give α = 0.01
Null models CSR on the network (binomial process on the network); planar CSR for comparison Network CSR accounts for the fact that accidents can only occur on roads

4.4 Analytical assumptions

Method Key assumption Reasonable here?
NKDE Accidents occur on the network, and network distance is the relevant distance Yes for accidents within 100 m of a major road; not for accidents on minor roads, which are excluded
Network K/G vs network CSR Accidents are equally likely anywhere on the network Partly: traffic volume varies strongly between roads, so clustering may reflect exposure rather than interaction
Planar KDE Accidents can occur anywhere in the window No: planar KDE spreads intensity into areas without roads
Planar G/L vs CSR Constant intensity across BMR No: intensity clearly varies, so clustering under planar CSR may just reflect uneven intensity
STKDE Spatial pattern changes smoothly over time Reasonable for seasonal change, but short holiday peaks are smoothed out
Space-time K-function Constant intensity in space and time No: like planar CSR, it cannot separate interaction from uneven intensity
All methods Recorded accidents represent all accidents No: data come from national road agencies, so city streets are likely under-recorded

NoteInterpretation

All 3,599 BMR accidents were reported by national road agencies: the Department of Highways (80.1%), the Expressway Authority of Thailand (13.1%) and the Department of Rural Roads (6.8%). None come from the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration, which manages most local streets in Bangkok. 6. First-order Point Patterns Analysis

6.1 Temporal variation

ggplot(acc_bmr_sf, aes(x = Month_num)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 1) +
  labs(x = "Month (1 = January)", y = "No. of accidents")

The red dashed lines mark the end of the New Year campaign (4 January, day 4), the Songkran “seven dangerous days” (11-17 April, days 101-107) and the start of the next New Year campaign (29 December, day 363).

ggplot(acc_bmr_sf, aes(x = DayofYear)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 1) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(4, 101, 107, 363),
             linetype = "dashed", colour = "red") +
  labs(x = "Day of year, 2022", y = "No. of accidents per day")
NoteInterpretation

Recorded accidents vary over time at two scales. Monthly counts range from about 210 in February to about 395 in October, with generally higher counts in the second half of 2022. Daily counts usually fall between 5 and 15, but the most prominent peaks coincide with the New Year and Songkran campaign periods, reaching about 36 accidents at the end of December. These peaks coincide with holiday travel periods, but the data alone cannot show why they occur. Counts are not adjusted for traffic volume, cover only recorded accidents on national roads, and come from a single year, so peaks indicate more recorded accidents rather than necessarily higher risk.

6.3 Network KDE (NKDE)

6.3.1 Preparing the lixels objects

Before computing NKDE, the network is cut into lixels. The lixel length is set to 500 m and the minimum length to 250 m, about half of the 1 km bandwidth.

lixels <- lixelize_lines(network,
                         500,
                         mindist = 250)

6.3.2 Generating line centre points

samples <- lines_center(lixels)

6.3.3 Performing NKDE

The quartic kernel and the simple method are used, as in the NetSPAA workflow. The bandwidth is 1 km, measured along the network. grid_shape = c(3, 3) splits the large BMR network into a 3 x 3 grid so that the calculation fits in memory; it does not change the result.

densities <- nkde(network,
                  events = acc_net,
                  w = rep(1, nrow(acc_net)),
                  samples = samples,
                  kernel_name = "quartic",
                  bw = 1000,
                  div = "bw",

                  digits = 1,
                  tol = 1,
                  grid_shape = c(3, 3),
                  max_depth = 8,
                  agg = 5,
                  sparse = TRUE,
                  verbose = FALSE)

7. Second-order Point Patterns Analysis

7.1 Network K- and G-function analysis

Research question: after accounting for the fact that accidents can only occur on the road network, are accidents clustered, regular or approximately random along the network?

Hypotheses:

H0: The observed accidents are uniformly distributed over the major road network in Bangkok.

H1: The observed accidents are not uniformly distributed over the major road network in Bangkok.

The CSR test is based on the binomial point process, which assumes accidents are randomly and independently distributed over the network. If H0 is rejected, accidents may be spatially interacting and dependent on each other, forming non-random patterns.

The analysis is restricted to Bangkok, because simulating random points on the full BMR network is very slow. kfunctions() computes both the network K-function and the network G-function, and compares them with 50 simulations of random points on the network.

bkk_sf <- bmr_sf %>%
  filter(shapeName == "Bangkok")

  filter(lengths(st_intersects(network, bkk_sf)) > 0)

acc_bkk <- acc_net %>%
  filter(lengths(st_intersects(acc_net, bkk_sf)) > 0)

9. Integrated Findings and Discussion

What does the first-order analysis reveal? Recorded accidents are highly uneven in space and time. NKDE shows that density is confined to a small number of specific roads, particularly corridors across southern Bangkok into Samut Prakan, into Pathum Thani and towards Samut Sakhon (Section 6.3); planar KDE shows the same corridors (Section 6.4). Temporally, counts are higher in the second half of 2022 (peaking in October) and spike during the New Year and Songkran campaign periods (Section 6.1), while the hotspot locations are stable through the year (Sections 6.5 and 6.6).

What does the second-order analysis reveal? Against planar CSR, accidents are strongly clustered at all distances tested (Section 7.2). Against CSR on the road network, .

How do they complement each other? The first-order results show that accidents are concentrated on a few roads, which explains much of the strong planar clustering. The network second-order test asks whether, along those roads, accidents are closer together than random placement on the network would produce.

Are the findings consistent across analytical views? The location of the main corridors is consistent across NKDE, planar KDE at 0.5-2 km bandwidths, and spatio-temporal KDE. The strength of clustering is not: it depends strongly on whether the null model is planar CSR or CSR on the network.

What assumptions or limitations matter? The most important are the observation process (only national road agencies’ records), the absence of traffic-volume data, the restriction of the network K-function to Bangkok, and sensitivity to bandwidth; these are detailed in Section 11.

Are there alternative explanations? Concentrations along corridors may reflect higher traffic volumes rather than higher risk per vehicle; network CSR assumes equal exposure on every road, which is unlikely. Repeated locations may partly reflect recording practices, such as locating accidents at fixed reference points, rather than true hotspots. Low intensity in inner Bangkok may reflect missing records from city-managed streets rather than safer roads.

10. Public-safety Planning and Management Implications

Supported directly by the analysis

Prioritising road segments rather than areas. NKDE shows that recorded accidents are concentrated on a small number of specific roads, particularly the corridors across southern Bangkok into Samut Prakan, the north–south corridor into Pathum Thani, and the south-western corridor towards Samut Sakhon. Road-safety audits, enforcement and engineering reviews can therefore be targeted at identified road segments rather than spread evenly across provinces.

Focusing on repeated locations. Some accidents share exact locations (Section 5.9). These locations are natural candidates for site-level investigation, such as junction design, signage and lighting.

Timing interventions. Accidents are more frequent in the second half of the year and during the New Year and Songkran periods. Enforcement and campaign resources can be scheduled to match these periods.

Monitoring emerging corridors. The south-western corridor towards Samut Sakhon becomes more prominent from mid-year onwards (Section 6.6), suggesting it should be monitored over future years to see whether this pattern persists.

Emergency response. Because accidents are concentrated along identifiable road segments, the locations of ambulance and rescue units can be evaluated against these segments, for example by checking response distances along the network.

Requiring additional information

Assessing risk rather than counts. The analysis identifies where recorded accidents concentrate, not where roads are most dangerous. Traffic volume data would be needed to convert counts into accident rates per vehicle-kilometre before concluding that a road segment is high-risk.

Explaining causes. The analysis does not show why accidents concentrate on these roads or at these times. Attributes such as presumed cause, vehicle type and road design, together with field inspections, would be needed before designing specific engineering or behavioural interventions.

Covering city streets. All records come from national road agencies. Integrating data from the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration would be necessary before drawing conclusions about inner Bangkok, where low intensity may reflect missing records.

Evaluating interventions. Multi-year data (for example, 2019–2022) would be needed to confirm whether the identified corridors and seasonal patterns are stable, and to evaluate whether past campaigns have had any effect.

11. Limitations

  • Observation process. All records come from national road agencies (Department of Highways 80.1%, Expressway Authority 13.1%, Department of Rural Roads 6.8%). Accidents on city-managed streets are likely missing, so patterns describe recorded accidents on these road types only.
  • No exposure data. Without traffic volumes, concentrations cannot be interpreted as higher risk, and network CSR assumes equal exposure on all roads.
  • Location quality. 189 records lacked coordinates, and some accidents share exact locations, possibly reflecting fixed reference points. These were kept, which inflates the planar G-function at distance zero.
  • Single year. Only 2022 is analysed, so patterns may not be stable across years.
  • Bandwidth sensitivity. Planar KDE and NKDE depend on bandwidth; results were compared at several bandwidths, but not all choices can be exhausted.
  • Limited simulations and extent. The network K-function uses 50 simulations and covers Bangkok only, for computational reasons.
  • Temporal smoothing. The day-of-year STKDE uses a 76-day temporal bandwidth, which smooths out short holiday peaks; the space–time K-function assumes constant intensity.
  • Network data. The OSM road network is a 2026 extract, whereas accidents are from 2022, and only major road classes were included. Roads built after 2022 appear in the network without any accidents.
  • Study window. BMR is defined by six provincial boundaries; a different definition of the metropolitan area could change edge effects and intensity estimates.
