Take-home Exercise 1: Geospatial Analytics for Public Safety

Spatial and Spatio-temporal Point Patterns of Road Traffic Accidents in the Bangkok Metropolitan Region, 2022

Author

Wang Xiaohan

Published

September 23, 2026

Modified

September 26, 2026

1. Introduction

Road traffic crashes are a major public-safety problem in Thailand. WHO estimates about 18,218 road deaths in 2021, a rate of 25.4 per 100,000 population and one of the highest in Asia, with motorcyclists accounting for most deaths. The Bangkok Metropolitan Region (BMR), comprising Bangkok and five vicinity provinces, is the country’s largest urban area, where crash patterns are likely to vary strongly across space and time. However, a concentration of crashes does not necessarily mean higher risk, as it may also reflect traffic volume, road density or how crashes are recorded. This study analyses road traffic accidents in BMR in 2022 using spatial and spatio-temporal point-pattern analysis. First-order analysis examines where and when accident intensity is highest; second-order analysis tests whether accidents cluster beyond this uneven intensity. A network-constrained analysis then checks whether these conclusions hold when accidents are restricted to the road network.

1.1 Research questions

  1. Where and when are road traffic accidents in BMR concentrated in 2022? (first-order)
  2. After accounting for this uneven intensity, do accidents show evidence of clustering, inhibition or approximate randomness? (second-order)
  3. Do the conclusions change when the analysis is constrained to the road network? (value-added)

4. Study Design

4.1 Point event definition

A point event is one recorded road traffic accident (one unique acc_code) that occurred between 1 January and 31 December 2022, located by its reported longitude and latitude, and falling inside BMR. Events are treated as an unmarked point pattern: attributes such as fatalities or vehicle type are not used as weights.

Issue Handling Section
Duplicate records Removed by unique acc_code 5.3
Repeated locations Jittered with rjitter() 5.9
Missing coordinates Removed 5.2
Swapped lat/long Corrected 5.2
Coordinates outside Thailand Removed 5.2
Outside BMR Removed with st_filter() 5.4
Outside 2022 Removed 5.1

4.2 Spatial and temporal study window

Item Decision Justification
Spatial window Union of 6 BMR provinces Operationalises “Bangkok Greater Metropolitan Area” with official provincial boundaries, giving a clear and reproducible extent
Temporal window 1 Jan - 31 Dec 2022 Required by the brief for the 2019-2022 dataset
Temporal unit Month (STKDE, 6.7); day of year (STKDE, 6.8 and 7.6); day and hour (graphics) Months give enough events per slice; day of year allows data-driven temporal bandwidths; day and hour reveal holiday and daily cycles

4.3 Methodological decisions

Decision Choice Justification
CRS WGS 84 / UTM 47N (EPSG:32647) Metric units for bandwidth and distance; zone 47N covers Bangkok
KDE kernel Gaussian Standard smooth kernel; results depend far more on bandwidth than on kernel choice
KDE bandwidth Compared bw.diggle, bw.ppl, bw.scott, fixed 0.5-5 km, adaptive Bandwidth controls the level of detail, so sensitivity is tested (Section 6)
Distance range 0-10 km Covers junction- to district-scale interaction while staying well below the width of BMR
Edge correction Border (CSR tests); modified border (inhomogeneous tests) Border is fast for thousands of points; the modified border is spatstat’s primary estimate for Linhom
Simulations 99 (CSR), 39 (inhomogeneous) Global envelopes with rank 1 give α = 0.01 and α = 0.025 respectively
Null models CSR; inhomogeneous Poisson CSR as a baseline; inhomogeneous Poisson separates interaction from uneven intensity (Section 7)

4.4 Analytical assumptions

Method Key assumption Reasonable here?
KDE Accidents can occur anywhere in the window No: accidents occur only on roads, so planar KDE spreads intensity into areas with no roads (addressed in Section 8)
L-function vs CSR Constant intensity across BMR No: intensity clearly varies, so clustering under CSR may just reflect uneven intensity
Inhomogeneous L-function KDE correctly estimates the true intensity Partly: results depend on bandwidth, so two bandwidths are compared
STKDE Spatial pattern changes smoothly over time Reasonable for seasonal change, but short holiday peaks are smoothed out (76-day bandwidth in 6.8)
Space-time K-function Constant intensity in space and time No: like CSR, it cannot separate interaction from uneven intensity
All methods Recorded accidents represent all accidents No: data come mainly from highway and rural road agencies, so city streets are likely under-recorded

9. Integrated Findings and Discussion

What does the first-order analysis reveal? Recorded accidents are highly uneven in space and time. Intensity is concentrated along a few linear corridors rather than broad areas, with the strongest hotspot in southern Bangkok near the Samut Prakan boundary (Section 6.6). Network KDE confirms that density is confined to specific roads (Section 8.3). Temporally, counts are higher in the second half of 2022 (peaking in October), spike during the New Year and Songkran campaign periods, and are highest from late afternoon to evening (Section 6.1).

What does the second-order analysis reveal? Against CSR, accidents are strongly clustered at all distances up to 10 km (Clark-Evans R = 0.30). Against an inhomogeneous Poisson model, most of this clustering disappears, but significant clustering remains below about 0.3 km when intensity is estimated with a 2 km bandwidth (Section 7.5).

How do they complement each other? The first-order results explain most of the apparent clustering: accidents cluster mainly because they are concentrated on a few corridors. The second-order results add that, within those corridors, accidents also recur within a few hundred metres, consistent with the 520 accidents sharing exact locations (Section 5.9).

Are the findings consistent across analytical views? The location of the main hotspot and corridors is robust across bandwidths (0.5–5 km), spatio-temporal KDE and network KDE. The strength of clustering is not: it depends on the null model and the bandwidth used to estimate intensity. [8.4: state whether the network K-function supports or contradicts the planar results.]

What assumptions or limitations matter? The most important are the observation process (only national road agencies’ records), the absence of traffic-volume data, and sensitivity to bandwidth; these are detailed in Section 11.

Are there alternative explanations? Concentrations along corridors may reflect higher traffic volumes rather than higher risk per vehicle. Repeated locations may partly reflect recording practices, such as locating accidents at fixed reference points, rather than true hotspots. Low intensity in inner Bangkok may reflect missing records from city-managed streets rather than safer roads.

10. Public-safety Planning and Management Implications

Supported directly by the analysis

Prioritising corridors rather than areas. Network KDE shows that recorded accidents are concentrated on a small number of specific roads, particularly the corridors across southern Bangkok into Samut Prakan, the north–south corridor into Pathum Thani, and the south-western corridor towards Samut Sakhon. Road-safety audits, enforcement and engineering reviews can therefore be targeted at identified road segments rather than spread evenly across provinces.

Focusing on repeated locations. The significant short-range clustering below about 0.3 km, together with 520 accidents sharing exact locations, indicates that some sites experience repeated accidents. These locations are natural candidates for site-level investigation, such as junction design, signage and lighting.

Timing interventions. Accidents are more frequent in the second half of the year, during the New Year and Songkran periods, and from late afternoon to evening, especially on Fridays. Enforcement and campaign resources can be scheduled to match these periods.

Monitoring emerging corridors. The south-western corridor towards Samut Sakhon becomes more prominent from mid-year onwards (Section 6.8), suggesting it should be monitored over future years to see whether this pattern persists.

Emergency response. Because accidents are concentrated along identifiable corridors, the locations of ambulance and rescue units can be evaluated against these corridors, for example by checking response distances along the network.

Requiring additional information

Assessing risk rather than counts. The analysis identifies where recorded accidents concentrate, not where roads are most dangerous. Traffic volume data would be needed to convert counts into accident rates per vehicle-kilometre before concluding that a corridor is high-risk.

Explaining causes. The analysis does not show why accidents concentrate on these roads or at these times. Attributes such as presumed cause, vehicle type and road design, together with field inspections, would be needed before designing specific engineering or behavioural interventions.

Covering city streets. All records come from national road agencies. Integrating data from the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration would be necessary before drawing conclusions about inner Bangkok, where low intensity may reflect missing records.

Evaluating interventions. Multi-year data (for example, 2019–2022) would be needed to confirm whether the identified corridors and seasonal patterns are stable, and to evaluate whether past campaigns have had any effect.

11. Limitations

  • Observation process. All records come from national road agencies (Department of Highways 80.1%, Expressway Authority 13.1%, Department of Rural Roads 6.8%). Accidents on city-managed streets are likely missing, so patterns describe recorded accidents on these road types only.
  • No exposure data. Without traffic volumes, concentrations cannot be interpreted as higher risk.
  • Location quality. 189 records lacked coordinates, and 520 accidents shared exact locations, possibly reflecting fixed reference points. Jittering removes duplicates but introduces small artificial displacements.
  • Single year. Only 2022 is analysed, so patterns may not be stable across years.
  • Bandwidth sensitivity. KDE maps and especially the inhomogeneous L-function depend on bandwidth; results were tested at several bandwidths but not all choices can be exhausted.
  • Intensity estimated from the same data. The inhomogeneous null model uses a KDE of the same accidents, which can absorb genuine clustering, and the two border-correction estimators gave different results.
  • Limited simulations. The inhomogeneous envelopes use 39 simulations, giving jagged envelopes and limited precision.
  • Temporal smoothing. The day-of-year STKDE uses a 76-day temporal bandwidth, which smooths out short holiday peaks; the space–time K-function assumes constant intensity.
  • Network data. The OSM road network is a 2026 extract, whereas accidents are from 2022, and only major road classes were included. The network K-function was restricted to Bangkok for computational reasons, and network KDE values are relative densities.
  • Study window. BMR is defined by six provincial boundaries; a different definition of the metropolitan area could change edge effects and intensity estimates.
